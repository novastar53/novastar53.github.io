The Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem¶

Introduction¶

Suppose we have a coin that comes up heads with probability 0.6. If we toss it once, we either get a head or a tail. If we toss it 25 times, we can calculate the fraction of heads. If we repeat that experiment thousands of times, we can plot the distribution of those fractions.

This gives us two interesting observations. As we increase the number of tosses in each experiment, the fraction of heads gets closer to 0.6. At the same time, the distribution of those fractions starts looking like a Gaussian. How can the distribution become more Gaussian while also getting narrower? And how can a Gaussian approximate a quantity that’s always between 0 and 1?

In this post, we’ll investigate these questions using a simple Bernoulli experiment. We’ll distinguish the distribution of individual observations from the distribution of their average, run simulations to see what changes when we increase the number of trials or the number of experiments, and then connect the results to the Law of Large Numbers (LLN) and the Central Limit Theorem (CLT).

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.stats import binom, norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
p = 0.6
plt.rcParams.update({'figure.figsize': (8, 4), 'axes.grid': True, 'grid.alpha': 0.18})

Bernoulli Rewards¶

Let’s start with the simplest possible reward distribution. Each observation \(X\) is either 0 or 1, with probability \(p\) of producing 1:

\[ X\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p),\qquad P(X=1)=p,\qquad P(X=0)=1-p. \]

We’ll use \(p=0.6\) throughout the experiments. The true mean and variance are

\[ \mu=E[X]=p=0.6,\qquad \sigma^2=p(1-p)=0.24. \]

The standard deviation is \(\sigma=\sqrt{0.24}\approx0.49\). Importantly, the mean is not itself a possible reward: each observation is still either 0 or 1. Let’s plot the reward distribution and mark its mean.

mu = p
sigma = np.sqrt(p * (1 - p))
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4))
ax.bar([0, 1], [1-p, p], width=0.23, color=['#5B8DB8', '#5B8DB8'])
ax.axvline(mu, color='#D97706', linestyle='--', linewidth=2, label=f'Mean μ = {mu:.2f}')
for x, prob in [(0, 1-p), (1, p)]:
    ax.text(x, prob + 0.02, f'{prob:.1f}', ha='center', fontsize=11)
ax.set(xlim=(-0.3, 1.3), ylim=(0, 0.77), xticks=[0, 1], xlabel='Reward X', ylabel='Probability', title='Individual reward: Bernoulli(0.6)')
ax.legend(title=f'SD σ = {sigma:.3f}')
plt.show()

From Bernoulli to Binomial¶

Now draw \(n\) independent rewards \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\), all with the same success probability \(p\). If we add them together, we count the number of successes:

\[ S_n=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p). \]

If we average instead of adding, we obtain the sample mean:

\[ \bar X_n=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i=\frac{S_n}{n}. \]

For \(n=25\), the sum can take any integer value from 0 to 25, while the average can take the 26 values \(0,1/25,2/25,\ldots,1\). These distributions contain the same information; the average just divides the horizontal axis by 25.

Let’s plot both for \(n=25\). The binomial plot counts successes; the scaled plot shows the corresponding fractions of successes.

n = 25
k = np.arange(n + 1)
probs = binom.pmf(k, n, p)
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 4))
axes[0].bar(k, probs, width=0.85, color='#5B8DB8')
axes[0].axvline(n*p, color='#D97706', linestyle='--', label=f'Mean = {n*p:g}')
axes[0].set(xlabel='Number of successes Sₙ', ylabel='Probability', title='Binomial sum: n = 25')
axes[0].legend()
axes[1].bar(k/n, probs, width=0.032, color='#5B8DB8')
axes[1].axvline(p, color='#D97706', linestyle='--', label=f'Mean = {p:.1f}')
axes[1].set(xlabel='Sample mean X̄ₙ = Sₙ/n', ylabel='Probability', title='Scaled binomial sample mean: n = 25')
axes[1].legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

One Experiment vs. Repeated Experiments¶

There are two different sample sizes involved in the simulations, and it’s easy to mix them up. We’ll call \(n\) the number of observations inside an experiment, and \(K\) the number of times we repeat that experiment.

One experiment with \(n=25\) produces 25 zeros and ones and one sample mean. We can plot the raw observations as two bars, but we cannot draw a distribution of means from just one realized mean.

To see that distribution empirically, we need to repeat the entire experiment. If we collect \(K\) sample means and plot their histogram, we are estimating the sampling distribution of the sample mean. For a fixed \(n\), its exact probabilities are

\[ P\left(\bar X_n=\frac{k}{n}\right)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k},\qquad k=0,\ldots,n. \]

Increasing \(K\) makes our histogram a better approximation to this fixed-\(n\) distribution. It does not change the distribution itself or make it more Gaussian.

Let’s deliberately choose a small \(n=5\) and compare 100, 10,000, and 1,000,000 repetitions. The black markers give the exact probabilities.

n = 5  # deliberately small to show that the sampling distribution stays discrete
repetitions = [100, 10_000, 1_000_000]
k = np.arange(n+1)
x = k/n
exact = binom.pmf(k, n, p)
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(14, 4), sharey=True)
for ax, K in zip(axes, repetitions):
    counts = rng.binomial(n, p, size=K)
    empirical = np.bincount(counts, minlength=n+1)/K
    ax.bar(x, empirical, width=.13, color='#5B8DB8', label='Observed frequency')
    ax.plot(x, exact, 'ko', markersize=5, label='Exact probability')
    ax.set(xlim=(-.1,1.1), xticks=x, xlabel='Sample mean', title=f'K = {K:,} experiments')
axes[0].set_ylabel('Probability')
axes[0].legend(fontsize=8)
fig.suptitle('Fixed n = 5: more repetitions do NOT turn the distribution Gaussian')
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Increasing the Number of Trials¶

Now let’s change \(n\) rather than \(K\). We’ll look at the exact sampling distribution of the mean for \(n=5,25,100,\) and \(1000\), keeping the reward probability fixed at \(p=0.6\).

Two things happen as \(n\) increases. First, there are more possible sample means: exactly \(n+1\), spaced \(1/n\) apart. Second, more of the probability mass moves close to the true mean, 0.6.

That’s an interesting combination. The set of possible values becomes denser in \([0,1]\), but the values we are likely to observe become increasingly concentrated around 0.6.

The next plot uses the same horizontal axis for all four distributions, so we can see the concentration directly.

ns = [5, 25, 100, 1000]
fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(12, 7), sharex=True)
for ax, n in zip(axes.flat, ns):
    k = np.arange(n + 1)
    x = k/n
    probabilities = binom.pmf(k, n, p)
    ax.vlines(x, 0, probabilities, color='#5B8DB8', linewidth=1.2)
    ax.axvline(p, linestyle='--', color='#D97706', linewidth=1.5)
    ax.set(xlim=(0,1), title=f'n = {n}, n+1 = {n+1} possible means', xlabel='Sample mean', ylabel='Probability at that mean')
fig.suptitle('Exact sampling distributions: unscaled sample means')
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

The Law of Large Numbers¶

The Law of Large Numbers formalizes the concentration we just observed. For independent, identically distributed observations with a finite mean \(\mu\), the weak law states that

\[ \bar X_n\xrightarrow{P}\mu. \]

In other words, for any fixed error tolerance \(\epsilon>0\),

\[ P(|\bar X_n-\mu|>\epsilon)\longrightarrow0\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty. \]

Why does the average become more stable? For independent observations, variances add. The variance of the sum is \(n\sigma^2\), but dividing the sum by \(n\) divides its variance by \(n^2\):

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)=\frac{1}{n^2}n\sigma^2=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}. \]

So the standard deviation of the sample mean, also called its standard error, is

\[ \operatorname{SE}(\bar X_n)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}. \]

For our Bernoulli example, this is \(\sqrt{0.24/n}\). As \(n\) increases, the spread shrinks to zero. In the limit, the distribution of the unscaled sample mean converges to a point mass at 0.6. Let’s plot how quickly the standard error decreases.

ns = np.array([5, 10, 25, 50, 100, 200, 500, 1000, 5000, 10000])
se = np.sqrt(p*(1-p)/ns)
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.plot(ns, se, marker='o', color='#5B8DB8')
ax.set_xscale('log')
ax.set(xlabel='Number of observations n (log scale)', ylabel='SD of the sample mean', title='Standard error → 0 as n increases')
plt.show()
print('n    standard error')
for n, s in zip(ns[[0,2,4,7,9]],se[[0,2,4,7,9]]):
    print(f'{n:<5d}{s:.5f}')

n    standard error
5    0.21909
25   0.09798
100  0.04899
1000 0.01549
100000.00490

The Central Limit Theorem¶

The Law of Large Numbers tells us that the sample mean approaches the true mean. It doesn’t tell us the shape of the remaining fluctuations. That’s where the Central Limit Theorem comes in.

For independent, identically distributed observations with finite mean \(\mu\) and finite, nonzero variance \(\sigma^2\), the CLT states that

\[ \boxed{Z_n=\frac{\bar X_n-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal N(0,1).} \]

Notice that the theorem is about the centered and standardized sample mean, not the raw observations and not the unscaled sample mean. We subtract \(\mu\) and divide by the standard error. This keeps the fluctuations on a comparable scale while their unscaled size shrinks.

For large but finite \(n\), the same result motivates the practical approximation

\[ \bar X_n\approx\mathcal N\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right). \]

For our coin, that becomes \(\mathcal N(0.6,0.24/n)\). It’s an approximation: the true distribution of \(\bar X_n\) is discrete for every finite \(n\).

Let’s plot the exact standardized distributions for increasing \(n\) against the standard Gaussian density. The plots use a common standardized horizontal axis. A discrete distribution’s probabilities are converted to comparable heights using their spacing on that axis.

ns = [5, 25, 100, 1000]
fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(12, 7), sharex=True, sharey=True)
zgrid = np.linspace(-4.2, 4.2, 501)
for ax, n in zip(axes.flat, ns):
    k = np.arange(n+1)
    z = (k/n - p) / np.sqrt(p*(1-p)/n)
    probability = binom.pmf(k, n, p)
    dz = 1 / np.sqrt(n*p*(1-p))
    ax.bar(z, probability/dz, width=dz*.85, alpha=.68, color='#5B8DB8', label='Exact scaled binomial')
    ax.plot(zgrid, norm.pdf(zgrid), color='#D97706', linewidth=2, label='N(0,1) density')
    ax.set(xlim=(-4.2,4.2), ylim=(0,.65), xlabel='Standardized mean Zₙ', ylabel='Density (probability / spacing)', title=f'n = {n}')
axes[0,0].legend(fontsize=8)
fig.suptitle('Standardized sample means approach a Gaussian shape')
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

How Can a Bounded Distribution Approach a Gaussian?¶

This is the part that initially seems impossible. Every Bernoulli sample mean satisfies \(0\leq\bar X_n\leq1\). A Gaussian assigns some probability outside any bounded interval. How can the former converge to the latter?

The distinction is between the unscaled sample mean and the standardized sample mean. The unscaled mean doesn’t converge to a non-degenerate Gaussian. Its distribution collapses to the true mean, as the Law of Large Numbers predicts.

The CLT instead concerns

\[ Z_n=\frac{\sqrt n(\bar X_n-0.6)}{\sqrt{0.24}}. \]

Because \(\bar X_n\) lies between 0 and 1, the range of \(Z_n\) is

\[ -\sqrt{1.5n}\leq Z_n\leq\sqrt{\frac{2n}{3}}. \]

For each finite \(n\), that range is bounded. But it is not bounded by the same endpoints for every \(n\). As \(n\to\infty\), the lower endpoint goes to \(-\infty\) and the upper endpoint to \(+\infty\).

More importantly, convergence in distribution doesn’t require matching supports at any finite \(n\). It means that the standardized cumulative distribution functions approach the Gaussian cumulative distribution function. A distribution supported on discrete points can converge in distribution to a continuous one.

Let’s calculate the minimum and maximum standardized sample means for a few values of \(n\).

ns = np.array([5, 25, 100, 1000, 10000])
low = -np.sqrt(1.5*ns)
high = np.sqrt(2*ns/3)
print(' n       min Zₙ       max Zₙ')
for n, a, b in zip(ns, low, high):
    print(f'{n:5d}   {a:10.2f}    {b:10.2f}')

Confidence Intervals and Reinforcement Learning¶

Why do we care about the CLT beyond its mathematical elegance? It gives us a way to quantify how much a sample mean might fluctuate across independent experiments.

Suppose we run a bandit algorithm \(n\) times independently and record one performance metric per run, such as cumulative reward. We calculate the empirical average \(\bar R_n\). If the metric has standard deviation \(\sigma\), the standard error is \(\sigma/\sqrt n\). We usually don’t know \(\sigma\), so we replace it with the sample standard deviation \(s\).

For sufficiently large samples, this leads to an approximate 95% confidence interval:

\[ \bar R_n\pm1.96\frac{s}{\sqrt n}. \]

The frequentist interpretation matters: if we repeated this procedure, approximately 95% of the resulting intervals would contain the fixed true expected reward. It does not mean the particular interval we observed assigns 95% probability to the unknown mean.

Let’s work through an example. Suppose 100 independent runs give an average reward of 5 and a sample standard deviation of 2. The estimated standard error is \(2/\sqrt{100}=0.2\), so the approximate interval is

\[ 5\pm1.96(0.2)=[4.608,5.392]. \]

The same reasoning helps when comparing RL algorithms. Rather than reporting that UCB achieved a higher average reward than epsilon-greedy, we can quantify the uncertainty in the difference. If both algorithms are evaluated on the same randomly generated bandit instances, we can compute the performance difference for each paired run and estimate the standard error of those differences. This often gives a cleaner comparison than treating the experiments as unrelated.

There’s also a limitation worth remembering: the usual formula assumes independent observations (or suitable dependence conditions). Consecutive rewards from an RL trajectory can be correlated. And a small standard error says nothing by itself about whether an estimator is biased.

Sampling Distributions vs. Bayesian Posteriors¶

This discussion also helps distinguish the CLT from Bayesian inference. In a frequentist sampling distribution, the true mean is fixed and the sample mean varies across hypothetical repetitions:

\[ \bar X_n\approx\mathcal N\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right). \]

In Bayesian inference, we instead specify a prior over the unknown parameter and update it with observed data. The result is a posterior \(p(\mu\mid D)\). This posterior describes uncertainty about the true mean given the particular dataset we observed.

The posterior need not be Gaussian. For Bernoulli rewards, a Beta prior produces a Beta posterior. In regular models with sufficient data, Bayesian posteriors often become approximately Gaussian, but that’s a different asymptotic result, associated with the Bernstein–von Mises theorem rather than the CLT itself.

Conclusion¶

We started with rewards that could only be 0 or 1. Summing them gave us a binomial random variable; averaging them gave us a scaled binomial random variable. Repeating the experiment allowed us to visualize the sampling distribution, but increasing the number of repetitions did not alter its shape for a fixed sample size.

Increasing the number of observations within each experiment did change the distribution. The number of possible sample means increased, while their probabilities concentrated near the true mean. The Law of Large Numbers explains that concentration. The Central Limit Theorem explains why the standardized fluctuations approach a Gaussian even as the unscaled distribution collapses to a point.

The two results answer different questions. The LLN tells us where the estimate goes; the CLT tells us how its errors behave on the way there. Together, they underpin confidence intervals, Monte Carlo estimation, and the statistical evaluation of reinforcement learning algorithms.
